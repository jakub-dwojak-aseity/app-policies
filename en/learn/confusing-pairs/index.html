Confusing pairs in Japanese: は or が, もう or まだ

Pairs that look interchangeable and are not – each sentence comes with the situation and the reason the other form fails.

  • あまり〜ない / ぜんぜん〜ない
  • で（場所） / で（手段）
  • 〜前に / 〜てから
  • 〜ませんか / 〜ましょう
  • もう / まだ
  • ね / よ
  • に（存在の場所） / で（場所）
  • 〜たい / 〜がほしい
  • 〜てから / 〜たあとで
  • 〜てもいいです / 〜なくてもいいです
  • と（並列） / や
  • は / が（新情報）
  • 〜より / いちばん

あまり〜ない / ぜんぜん〜ない

Both go with a negative, but they differ in strength. あまり softens: “not very”, so a little after all. ぜんぜん seals it: “not at all”, not one bit. Getting this wrong can cost you socially — ぜんぜん where あまり belonged sounds like cutting the conversation short.

Examples

  • You understand simple sentences and know some words, but free conversation is beyond you. You want to answer modestly without closing the subject.日本語はあまり分かりません。You do understand something, so this is about softening, not about sealing the matter. ぜんぜん would mean you do not understand a single word.
  • You do not drink alcohol at all — not a glass, ever. You are declining so that nobody tries to top you up.お酒はぜんぜん飲みません。The negation is total. あまり飲みません would mean “I rarely drink”, that is, that you do drink — and the other person would happily pour you another.

で（場所） / で（手段）

The same particle handles two entirely different roles. With a place it answers “where”, with a tool it answers “with what”. The spelling is identical, so the only thing that settles it is the word it follows.

〜前に / 〜てから

Both constructions put two events in order, but they look at them from opposite ends. 前に names the action that comes later and says what precedes it. てから names the earlier action and says what follows it. The form is fixed: before 前に the verb always stands in the dictionary form.

Examples

  • You are describing an evening habit: brushing your teeth comes before sleeping.寝る前に歯をみがきます。The point of reference is sleeping, and brushing one’s teeth precedes it. 寝てから would mean brushing one’s teeth after falling asleep.
  • You are setting a condition: play only once the homework is done.宿題をしてから、遊びます。The homework is to be closed off before the playing starts. する前に would reverse the order and have one play before doing it.

〜ませんか / 〜ましょう

Both forms propose acting together, but they assume a different state of the conversation. ませんか floats an idea the listener does not know yet and leaves them free to decline. ましょう assumes the listener agrees and moves on to acting. Thrown out as a first suggestion to someone who knows nothing about it, it sounds imposing.

Examples

  • You have just had the idea of lunch together. The listener has not heard of it yet.いっしょに昼ごはんを食べませんか。The suggestion is being made for the first time, so the listener needs a way out. 食べましょう would settle the matter before anyone had agreed.
  • The outing has already been agreed on. You are signaling that it is time to move.そろそろ出かけましょう。The agreement has already been reached, so a question would be a step backwards. ましょう simply puts the arrangement into effect.

もう / まだ

A pair about whether something has managed to happen. もう goes with the ました form: the action is closed. まだ goes with the ていません form: a state of not-yet lasting up to now. The commonest mistake is まだ食べませんでした — the negative of もう食べました is not the past but a state that is still going on.

Examples

  • Someone suggests having lunch together, but you ate half an hour ago.昼ごはんはもう食べました。The action is closed, and that closing is the whole content of the answer. まだ食べました does not make a well-formed sentence — まだ goes with a negative.
  • The teacher asks about your homework. You started it yesterday, but it is still not finished.宿題はまだ終わっていません。The state of not-yet lasts up to now. もう終わっていません would give “it has already stopped being finished”, which is not what you want to say.

ね / よ

Both stand at the end of a sentence and both disappear in an English translation, yet they do opposite things. ね assumes the listener thinks the same and asks for confirmation. よ hands them something they did not know. The deciding question: is this information already on their side, or are you only now passing it on.

Examples

  • You are standing together at a bus stop in full sun, wiping the sweat off your foreheads.今日は暑いですね。The listener feels exactly the same, so you are seeking agreement. よ would be adding information they supposedly lack — while they are standing beside you in the same heat.
  • The listener has their back to the tracks, busy with their phone, and has not noticed the train pull in.電車が来ましたよ。You are supplying information the listener does not have. ね would presuppose they already know and are only waiting for confirmation.

に（存在の場所） / で（場所）

Both particles mark a place, but they answer different questions. に says where something is located — nothing is happening there. で says where something takes place. The verb settles it: with existence and residence always に, with any action で.

Examples

  • You are saying what is lying on the desk. This describes a location, not an action.つくえの上に本があります。あります describes an object simply being there. The book is not doing anything, so the place is not the stage of an action and で would be wrong.
  • You are saying where you study. Studying is an action spread over time.図書館で勉強します。勉強します is an action, and the library is where it is carried out. 図書館に would mean a direction or a location, not the stage of the studying.

〜たい / 〜がほしい

Both constructions speak of the speaker’s wish, but they concern different things. たい refers to an action and attaches to a verb. ほしい refers to an object and follows a noun. The test is simple: do you want to do something, or to have something.

Examples

  • You are talking about a dream of traveling — the point is the act of going.日本へ行きたいです。The wish concerns the action of going, so たい attaches to the verb. ほしい requires a noun and is ungrammatical after a verb.
  • You are saying what object you would like to be given.新しい自転車がほしいです。A bicycle is a thing to possess, not an action. をしたい brings in a verb, する, that the sentence never had, and makes it “do a bicycle” — the point is to have one.

〜てから / 〜たあとで

Both put two actions in order, and in many sentences they are interchangeable. The difference lies in how firmly they bind: てから presents the first action as a condition or a starting point for the second, while あとで simply places the second later. When the order matters in practice, てから is the more natural choice.

〜てもいいです / 〜なくてもいいです

Both constructions grant latitude, but in opposite directions. てもいい says one is allowed to do something. なくてもいい says one does not have to do it. The first lifts a prohibition, the second lifts an obligation.

Examples

  • You are asking museum staff whether photography is permitted.ここで写真をとってもいいですか。The question is about permission to do something. とらなくてもいい would ask whether one is allowed not to take photographs — and nobody was requiring that.
  • You are reassuring a guest who cannot manage to finish their portion.全部食べなくてもいいですよ。The point is to lift the obligation to eat it all. 食べてもいい would sound like permission to eat, which the guest did not need at all.

と（並列） / や

と lists everything there is — the list is closed. や names examples and leaves the list open, so the listener fills in the rest. The deciding test: do you mean “exactly these”, or “things like these”.

Examples

  • You are describing from memory what was lying on the desk. You are not trying to name everything — you give the two things you remember.机の上に本やノートがありました。You are listing by way of example, so the list stays open. と would mean there were exactly two things on the desk and nothing besides.
  • You tip the contents of the bag onto the table and state exactly what was in it: a wallet and keys. Nothing else.かばんの中にはさいふとかぎがありました。The list is complete and closed. や would suggest there was something else in the bag that you are not naming.

は / が（新情報）

は announces a topic the listener already knows about, and hands the stress to what comes after it. が marks the subject as new information and holds the attention on it. A simple test: if you are answering a “who?” or “what?” question, the answer is the new part and takes が.

Examples

  • You are introducing yourself to someone you have just met. The listener already knows you are the one being talked about.わたしは銀行員です。The speaker is no news here — what is new is that they work at a bank. わたしが would mean “I am the one who is the bank employee”, as if someone had asked who that was.
  • You walk into a room and notice something the listener does not know about yet.あそこに猫がいます。The cat comes up in the conversation for the first time, so it is new information. 猫は would presuppose that the listener already knows of some cat, and would sound like a comparison with something else.

〜より / いちばん

より sets exactly two things side by side and marks which comes out higher. いちばん picks one out of a whole set. The number of items being compared settles it: two, or more.

Examples

  • You are comparing two means of transport.電車はバスより速いです。Two things are being set side by side, and いちばん requires a whole set to choose from. With two items より is the natural choice.
  • You are picking your favorite season out of all four.四季の中で秋がいちばん好きです。The set has four members, so a superlative is what is meant. より would have to name one particular season to compare against.
