Tiredness and sleep – Japanese mimetics

へとへと, くたくた, うとうと – six Japanese words for being worn out and for dozing off, with example sentences.

  • くたくた · to be worn out; of a thing: gone limp
  • へとへと · to be utterly drained
  • うとうと · to doze off
  • すやすや · to sleep peacefully, breathing evenly
  • よろよろ · to totter along
  • ぱんぱん · to be swollen tight

くたくた · to be worn out; of a thing: gone limp

Limpness from wear: a body, but also a thing, that has lost its firmness.

The limpness sits in the root くた (as in くたびれる, “to tire out, to wear out”), not in the voiceless く itself. The same root with the 濁点 stands in ぐったり, but what separates it from くたくた is not the diacritic — it is the shape: AっBり shows a body that has already gone slack, plain to see from outside, and it attaches with する／と, not だ／になる. Hence くたくたのシャツ and くたくたに煮る: the same word describes a worn-out thing, and へとへと is never said of a thing at all.

Examples

  • 破やぶれはないが、シャツの張はりが抜ぬけてくたくただ。The shirt isn't torn, just limp and shapeless.
  • 茹ゆですぎて、ほうれん草そうがくたくたになった。I overboiled the spinach and it went all limp.

へとへと · to be utterly drained

Exhaustion at the very end of your strength: still on your feet, but only just.

The mark exists, the pair does not: the 濁点 gives べとべと, but that is a separate word — about stickiness, not tiredness. Here the exhaustion is carried by the repeated ABAB shape itself. What is restricted is the attachment: だ and になる, never する — 彼はへとへとだった is correct, 彼はへとへとする is not. Its neighbour ぐったり speaks of a body that has already gone slack (力なく横たわる, うなだれる); as long as you are still on your feet and moving, it is へとへと, not ぐったり.

Examples

  • 引ひっ越こしで疲つかれ、体力たいりょくを使つかい果はたしてへとへとだ。The move has drained me; I'm utterly spent.
  • 子守こもりに疲つかれ、気力きりょくも尽つきてへとへとになった。Minding the kids drained every last drop of me.

うとうと · to doze off

Sleep you keep falling out of.

The ABAB shape means “over and over” here — dozing off and waking. The AっBり shape of this root is うっとり — and it means something else: rapture, being entranced, not sleep. Sleep slept through is a separate word, ぐっすり. すやすや speaks of even, quiet breathing, that is of sleep that lasts; うとうと of sleep that keeps breaking.

Examples

  • 心地ここちよい眠気ねむけで、だんだんうとうとしてきた。Pleasantly drowsy, I gradually begin to doze off.
  • 心地ここちよい午後ごご、何度なんどもうとうとと船ふねをこいだ。On a pleasant afternoon I kept nodding off.

すやすや · to sleep peacefully, breathing evenly

The even, quiet breathing of a sleeper.

Voiceless す keeps the breathing even and quiet. What separates its neighbours is not a diacritic but the sleep itself: うとうと keeps breaking, ぐっすり is deep and slept right through, and すやすや says nothing about depth — which is why it fits a light sleep too, as long as the breathing stays even. The word itself lives on a verb — すやすや眠る, never すやすやする.

Examples

  • 赤あかちゃんが規則正きそくただしい寝息ねいきですやすやと眠ねむる。The baby sleeps peacefully, breathing evenly.
  • 子こどもがすやすやと寝息ねいきを立たてている。The child is breathing softly and evenly in sleep.

よろよろ · to totter along

No strength left in the legs, and every step is close to a fall.

There is no voiced partner here. The dictionary describes this word in a single line — 力なく倒れそうになりながら歩くさま — and that is all of it: walking with a body that has run out of strength. Nothing about a destination; 酔ってよろよろと歩き回る is an ordinary sentence. So よろよろ is simply narrower than ふらふら: ふらふら takes the same scene and adds dizziness (熱でふらふらする) and aimlessness itself (目的もなくふらふらする) — よろよろ says nothing about either, because it speaks only of the walk.

Examples

  • 疲つかれ果はてた足あしで、よろよろとゆっくり歩あるき続つづけた。On exhausted legs I kept tottering slowly on.
  • 重おもい荷物にもつに疲つかれ、一歩一歩いっぽいっぽよろよろしながら進すすむ。Worn out by the heavy load, I totter step by step.

ぱんぱん · to be swollen tight

Legs or a belly so full they might burst.

The 半濁点 gives fullness stretched to its limit. The 濁点 form ばんばん would be banging, not a state.

Examples

  • 歩あるき疲つかれて、ふくらはぎが張はってぱんぱんだ。Tired from walking, my calves are swollen tight.
  • 立たち仕事しごとで疲つかれ、足あしがむくんでぱんぱんになる。Standing at work tires me; my legs swell up tight.
