Sound pairs: こ–と – Japanese mimetics

ころころ and ごろごろ, とんとん and どんどん – pairs from こ to と where you hear what knocks, rolls or rustles.

  • こそこそ · to do something furtively, shiftily
  • ごそごそ · to rummage
  • ころころ · to roll lightly
  • ごろごろ · to roll heavily
  • さわさわ · to rustle softly
  • ざわざわ · to buzz with restless noise
  • しゃりしゃり · to crunch juicily, with a grain of ice
  • じゃりじゃり · to grate with grit
  • とんとん · to tap lightly
  • どんどん · to bang loudly; more and more

こそこそ · to do something furtively, shiftily

Sneaking, with a guilty look: whispered scheming, carrying something out the back door.

こそこそ names a purpose: not to be noticed — and it passes judgement while doing so, because it looks like something dishonest. こっそり carries no such verdict: こっそり贈り物を用意する is a kind surprise, while こそこそ贈り物を用意する sounds shifty. ひそひそ is only about the voice, lowered near other people, whereas こそこそ covers the whole behaviour, including when nobody is speaking at all. ごそごそ names a sound, that of things being shifted about — 濁点 intensifies nothing here; these are simply two words sharing a skeleton.

Examples

  • 後うしろめたそうに何なにかこそこそしている。Looking guilty, he's doing something on the sly.
  • 夫おっとは後うしろめたげに足音あしおとを忍しのばせこそこそしている。Looking guilty, my husband creeps about on tiptoe.

ごそごそ · to rummage

Hunting for keys in a bag, digging through a box.

ごそごそ is rummaging: hands shift things and those things rustle. It bothers nobody — and that is what separates it from がさがさ, which grates on the ear. こそこそ, by contrast, is no sound at all but a purpose: doing something so that nobody notices. You may rummage ごそごそ in plain sight.

Examples

  • 押おし入いれで何なにかが小ちいさくゆっくりごそごそと動うごく。Something in the closet shuffles, slow and soft.
  • 粗あらい麻袋あさぶくろの中なかをそっとごそごそしている。Someone rummages quietly in a coarse hemp sack.

ころころ · to roll lightly

Something small and light rolls along.

Voiceless こ keeps the rolling thing hand-sized; ころころ rolls a sweet or a pebble, and it takes ごろごろ with 濁点 to swap it for a boulder you can hear coming.

Examples

  • 小ちいさくて軽かるい飴玉あめだまが机つくえの上うえをころころ転ころがった。Small, light candies rolled across the table.
  • 軽かるいビー玉だまが坂さかをころころと転ころがっていく。A light marble rolls down the slope.

ごろごろ · to roll heavily

Something big and heavy rolls with a rumble.

Voiced ご makes the same thing bigger, heavier and less pleasant: ころころ rolls what fits in your hand, ごろごろ a boulder you can hear. ゴゴゴ rolls nothing at all — it is a katakana mark from a comic panel, an ominous rumble warning that something is coming, not a word anybody says. Note: ごろごろする means “to laze about” (休日は家でごろごろしている) and that is its commonest use today — rolling is ごろごろと転がる.

Examples

  • 大おおきくて重おもい岩いわが山道やまみちをごろごろ転ころがってきた。A big, heavy boulder came rolling down the trail.
  • 重おもい丸太まるたが荷台にだいの上うえでごろごろと転ころがる。A heavy log rolls about on the truck bed.

さわさわ · to rustle softly

Leaves and ears of rice swaying in a gentle breeze.

Voiceless さ keeps the sound edgeless and so quiet it does not carry a step further — it is background, not an event; ざわざわ lifts that same murmur far enough that it stops being background and starts getting in the way. さらさら is said of leaves too (笹の葉さらさら), and the line between them does not run along water: 小川がさらさら流れる is an ordinary sentence. It runs along the sound and what is moving: さらさら is the dry, light rustle of thin things slipping over one another (bamboo leaves, hair, sand, a stream), while さわさわ is the soft murmur of many living green leaves or ears of grain that a gentle wind sways where they stand (風で木の葉がさわさわと鳴る). The word lives only with a verb (さわさわと鳴る, さわさわと揺れる); さわさわする is not used.

Examples

  • 青あおい若葉わかばが、風かぜにさわさわと揺ゆれる。Fresh green leaves rustle in the wind.
  • 穏おだやかな風かぜに、青あおい稲穂いなほがさわさわと揺ゆれる。Green ears of rice rustle in the mild breeze.

ざわざわ · to buzz with restless noise

A hall before curtain-up, a crowd after bad news.

Voiced ざ coarsens the sound and raises the volume: さわさわ does not carry a step further, ざわざわ fills a whole hall. It does not swap leaves for people — 木々がざわざわ揺れる is an ordinary sentence. そわそわ is no sound at all: it is one person's restlessness, unable to sit still because something is about to happen, whereas ざわざわ is heard.

Examples

  • 大勢おおぜいの客きゃくの低ひくい話はなし声ごえが、ざわざわと広ひろがる。A crowd's low murmur spreads through the room.
  • 開演前かいえんまえ、大勢おおぜいの話はなし声ごえで会場かいじょうがざわざわしている。Before curtain-up the hall buzzes with voices.

しゃりしゃり · to crunch juicily, with a grain of ice

Shaved ice, sorbet, frozen fruit.

Voiceless し gives a fine, wet grain — which is to say, tasty. じゃりじゃり is those same grains dried out: sand that grinds between the teeth and nobody eats. しゃきしゃき has no grain at all — it is the firmness of fibre in fresh cabbage or lettuce — whereas しゃりしゃり always carries a crystal of ice or the flesh of frozen fruit.

Examples

  • 果汁かじゅうたっぷりのシャーベットをしゃりしゃりと食たべる。I crunch through a juicy fruit sorbet.
  • 凍こおらせたぶどうは、甘あまくてしゃりしゃりだ。Frozen grapes are sweet and icy-crunchy.

じゃりじゃり · to grate with grit

Sand between the teeth, gravel in a shoe.

Voiced じ dries those same grains out: the wet, tasty flesh of しゃりしゃり turns into dry sand that grinds between the teeth. ざらざら is not loose grit but the grain of a surface: it is said of a wall or of sandpaper the hand runs over, while じゃりじゃり is said of grains that move by themselves — in a shoe or between the teeth.

Examples

  • 乾かわいた砂粒すなつぶが奥歯おくばでじゃりじゃりと鳴なった。Dry grains of sand grated between my back teeth.
  • 靴くつに入はいった細こまかい砂すながじゃりじゃりと擦すれる。Fine sand in the shoe grinds and chafes.

とんとん · to tap lightly

A finger on a door, a knife on a board.

Voiceless と keeps the hand light and the sound quiet: the same knocking, but with a fingertip — audible only close up, where どんどん is a fist heard along the whole floor.

Examples

  • 指先ゆびさきで軽かるく戸とをとんとんとノックした。He knocked lightly on the door with a fingertip.
  • 空からの桶おけを指先ゆびさきで軽かるくとんとんと叩たたく。You tap the empty tub lightly with a fingertip.

どんどん · to bang loudly; more and more

A fist on a door, a drum — and prices that climb by the day.

Voiced ど does not change the motion — it changes the force: a light tap becomes a pounding. The second sense of どんどん is the commoner one today: the adverb “more and more” (気温がどんどん上がる, 値段がどんどん上がる), where there is no hand and no person at all — a temperature or a price can climb that way. Pounding is what this card teaches. とんとん has senses of its own — 収支がとんとん (“to break even”) and とんとん拍子 (“smoothly, one after another”) — but “more and more” is not among them.

Examples

  • 返事へんじがないので、拳こぶしで戸とをどんどんと叩たたき続つづけた。No answer, so I kept pounding the door.
  • 毎朝まいあさの合図あいずに大太鼓おおだいこがどんどんと鳴なる。Every morning the big drum booms as a signal.
